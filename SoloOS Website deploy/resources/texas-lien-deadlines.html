Texas lien deadlines: the date your rights end, and how to count to it.

If you are a subcontractor or supplier on a private Texas job and you have not been paid, there is a date after which you cannot do anything about it. Not harder. Over.

Not legal advice. This is research on how the deadlines are counted, written so you can ask your attorney the right questions. Chapter 53 is unforgiving and the facts of your job matter. Confirm your dates with a Texas construction attorney before you rely on them.

If you are a subcontractor or a supplier on a private job in Texas and you have not been paid, there is a date on the calendar after which you cannot do anything about it. Not a date where your claim gets harder. A date where your lien rights are gone.

This page lays out those dates, with the statute next to each one.

Short version: on a commercial job, your notice of claim is due the 15th day of the third month after the month you did the work. On a residential job, it is the 15th day of the second month. Those are different deadlines for the same unpaid invoice, and which one applies depends on a definition most people get wrong.

The notice deadline — Texas Property Code §53.056

A claimant who is not the original contractor must send a notice of claim for unpaid labor or materials to the owner and to the original contractor. Both. The statute reads:

"(a-1) For all unpaid labor or materials provided, the claimant must send a notice of claim for unpaid labor or materials to the owner or reputed owner and the original contractor. The notice must be sent: (1) for projects other than residential construction projects, not later than the 15th day of the third month after the month during which: (A) the labor or materials were provided... (2) for residential construction projects, not later than the 15th day of the second month after the month during which: (A) the labor or materials were provided..."

Worked examples

You did the work in Commercial — notice due Residential — notice due

The part people miss: it counts per month, not per job

The deadline runs from "the month during which the labor or materials were provided." If you worked a job for five months and were never paid, that is five separate notice deadlines, not one. A single notice sent in month five does not obviously reach back and rescue month one — and no court has clearly resolved whether it does.

Track it month by month. One row per calendar month you were on that job.

Residential or not? This is where claims die

"Residential construction project" is a defined term, and the definition runs through two more definitions to get there.

"Residence" means the real property and improvements for a single-family house, duplex, triplex, or quadruplex or a unit in a multiunit structure used for residential purposes in which title to the individual units is transferred to the owners under a condominium or cooperative system...

"Residential construction contract" means a contract between an owner and a contractor in which the contractor agrees to construct or repair the owner's residence...

"Residential construction project" means a project for the construction or repair of a new or existing residence... as provided by a residential construction contract.

Two consequences worth knowing:

An apartment building is not residential. Five or more units is not a "residence" under that definition. So a 200-unit apartment complex gets the commercial deadlines — the longer ones. People assume the opposite constantly, because it is obviously a building people live in.

The test runs off the owner-to-contractor contract. Your own subcontract is not a "residential construction contract." The question is what the owner signed with the general contractor.

The lien affidavit deadline — §53.052

The notice is not the lien. Filing the affidavit is.

"(b) Except as provided by Subsection (c) or (d), a claimant other than an original contractor claiming the lien must file an affidavit with the county clerk not later than the 15th day of the fourth month after the later of: (1) the month the claimant last provided labor or materials..."

"(c) A claimant other than an original contractor claiming a lien arising from a residential construction project must file an affidavit with the county clerk not later than the 15th day of the third month after the later of: (1) the month the claimant last provided labor or materials..."

  • Commercial: 15th day of the fourth month after the month you last worked.
  • Residential: 15th day of the third month.
  • Filed in the county where the improvements are located, per §53.052(e).

And then, within five days of filing, §53.055 requires you to send a copy of the affidavit to the owner — and because you are not the original contractor, to the original contractor as well. This one gets missed constantly because it feels like the job is finished once the affidavit is on record.

Retainage is a separate clock

If your contract provides for retainage and that retainage is not already covered by your §53.056 notice, §53.057 requires its own notice:

"(a-1) The claimant must send the notice of claim for unpaid retainage to the owner or reputed owner and the original contractor not later than the earlier of: (1) the 30th day after the date the claimant's contract is completed, terminated, or abandoned; or (2) the 30th day after the date the original contract is terminated or abandoned."

Read prong (2) carefully. It says terminated or abandoned. It does not say completed. If the original contract simply finished normally, prong (2) never triggers at all, and prong (1) controls. A tracker that computes both and blindly takes the earlier will hand you a wrong date.

There is also a second, much shorter affidavit path for retainage. Under §53.103, a lien on reserved funds requires an affidavit not later than the 30th day after the earliest of completion, termination, or abandonment of the original contract. That is dramatically shorter than the §53.052(d) route. Watch both.

How to send it — and why certified mail specifically

§53.003(b) allows three methods: in person, certified mail, or a traceable private carrier that can confirm proof of receipt.

Use certified mail, and here is the concrete reason:

"(c) If notice is sent by certified mail, deposit or mailing of the notice in the United States mail in the form required constitutes compliance with the notice requirement."

Depositing certified mail is compliance. The date you mail it is the date that counts. A private courier has no equivalent rule in the statute — so with a courier, the notice has to actually be received by the deadline. That is a materially shorter runway for the same deadline.

The weekend rule, and why you should ignore it

§53.003(e) extends a deadline that lands on a Saturday, Sunday, or legal holiday to the next day that is not one.

Do not plan around it. "Legal holiday" is not defined in Chapter 53, Texas distinguishes between state holidays, optional holidays and partial-staffing holidays, and the county clerk's office may be closed on a day the extension may or may not cover. Treat it as a safety net you never intend to land in.

If the prime contract was signed before January 1, 2022

House Bill 2237 rewrote these rules effective January 1, 2022. It applies only to an original contract entered into on or after that date — and "original contract" means the contract between the owner and the general contractor, not your subcontract or your purchase order.

So a sub who signed a subcontract in 2024, on a job whose prime contract was signed in December 2021, is still governed by the old law — including the old two-notice system and the old accrual-of-indebtedness rules that were repealed.

Most subcontractors do not know the prime contract date. Ask the general contractor in writing, and keep the answer.

Homestead jobs have an extra requirement that is not yours to control

On a homestead, §53.254(g) requires specific statutory language to be included with or attached to your notice. Without it, the lien is not valid.

Worse, §53.254(a)–(e) requires the owner and the original contractor to have signed a written contract before any work began, signed by both spouses if the owner is married, and filed with the county clerk. Your rights as a sub flow through that contract. If the general contractor did not handle it properly, no amount of perfect deadline-hitting saves your lien.

Verify it before you rely on any date.

Public jobs are a different statute entirely

If the project is government-owned, Chapter 53 does not apply. There is no mechanic's lien on public property — the payment bond is the remedy, under Texas Government Code Chapter 2253, with different deadlines, different recipients, and a required sworn statement of account. A lower-tier claimant on a public job can have notice obligations in both the second and third month.

Do not use a Chapter 53 calendar on a public job.
